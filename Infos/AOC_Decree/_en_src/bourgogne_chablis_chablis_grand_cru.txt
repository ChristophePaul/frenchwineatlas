# CHAPTER I
## I. – Name of the designation
Only wines meeting the specific provisions set out below may claim the controlled designation of origin “Chablis grand cru”, initially recognised by the decree of 13 January 1938.
## II. – Geographical denominations and complementary terms
The name of the controlled designation of origin may be followed by the name of one of the following climats, for wines meeting the production conditions laid down in the present specification for the indication of one of these names:
- “Blanchot”;
- “Bougros”;
- “Grenouilles”;
- “Les Clos”;
- “Preuses”;
- “Valmur”;
- “Vaudésir”.
## III. – Colour and types of product
The controlled designation of origin “Chablis grand cru” is reserved for still white wines.
## IV. – Areas and zones in which the various operations are carried out
### 1° – Geographical area
The harvesting of the grapes, the vinification, the elaboration and the ageing of the wines are carried out within the territory of the commune of Chablis in the Yonne department.
The cartographic documents representing the geographical area may be consulted on the website of the National Institute of Origin and Quality.
### 2° – Delimited parcel area
a) – The wines come exclusively from vines situated within the delimited parcel production area as approved by the National Institute of Origin and Quality at the session of the competent national committee of 30 November 2023.
The National Institute of Origin and Quality lodges with the town hall of the commune referred to in 1° the graphic documents establishing the parcel boundaries of the production area thus approved.
b) – Where the name of the controlled designation of origin is followed by the name of a climat, the wines come exclusively from vines situated within the specific delimited parcel production area as approved by the National Institute of Origin and Quality at the session of the competent national committee of 30 November 2023.
The National Institute of Origin and Quality lodges with the town hall of the commune referred to in 1° the graphic documents establishing the parcel boundaries of the production area thus approved.

## V. – Grape varieties
The wines come exclusively from the chardonnay B variety.
## VI. – Management of the vineyard
### 1° – Training methods
a) – Planting density
- The vines have a minimum planting density of 5,500 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows less than or equal to 1.20 metres, except for vines planted on slopes of 40 % or more, for which this spacing is less than or equal to 1.60 metres;
- The vines have a spacing between plants within the same row greater than or equal to 0.80 metre.
b) – Pruning rules
The wines come from vines pruned according to the following provisions:
| GENERAL PROVISIONS |
| The vines are pruned with a maximum of 14 free eyes per plant and a maximum of 10 free eyes per square metre: – either short pruning (vines trained as Royat cordon); – or long pruning (single and double Guyot pruning, and Chablis pruning). |
| **PARTICULAR PROVISION** |
| Whatever the pruning method, the vines may be pruned with additional free eyes provided that, at the phenological stage corresponding to 11 or 12 leaves, the number of fruit-bearing shoots of the year per plant is less than or equal to the number of free eyes defined in the pruning rules. |
c) – Trellising and foliage height rules
- The height of trellised foliage is at least equal to 0.6 times the spacing between rows, the height of trellised foliage being measured between the lower limit of the foliage, set at least 0.30 metre above the ground, and the upper trimming limit;
- The vines must be trellised and the trellising is maintained.
d) – Average maximum load per parcel
The average maximum load per parcel is set at 9,500 kilograms per hectare.
e) – Threshold of missing vines
The percentage of dead or missing vine plants referred to in Article D. 645-4 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code is set at 20 %.
f) – Cultivation condition of the vine
The parcels are managed so as to ensure a good overall cultivation condition of the vine, reflected in particular by:
- control of a good sanitary condition, making it possible to obtain healthy foliage and healthy berries;
- upkeep of the soil, namely control of grass cover by a grass height of less than half the trellising height (height between the ground and the upper trellising wire), and control of erosion through the absence of visible roots.
### 2° – Other cultivation practices
a) – In order to preserve the characteristics of the physical and biological environment, which constitutes a fundamental element of the terroir:
- Permanent grass cover of the headlands and banks is compulsory; destruction of the plant cover is tolerated occasionally during works to bring soil back up the slope;
- Any substantial modification of the morphology, the subsoil, the arable layer or the elements that guarantee the integrity and sustainability of the soils of a parcel intended for the production of the controlled designation of origin is prohibited, with the exception of classic deep-ploughing works.
b) – Vine plantings and replacements are carried out with healthy plant material that has undergone hot-water treatment or any other method making it possible to combat flavescence dorée.
### 3° – Irrigation
Irrigation is prohibited.

### 2° – Entry into production of young vines
The benefit of the controlled designation of origin may only be granted to wines coming from:
- parcels of young vines only from the 2nd year following that in the course of which planting was carried out in place before 31 July;
- parcels of young vines only from the 1st year following that in the course of which grafting in place was carried out before 31 July;

## IX. – Processing, elaboration, ageing, packaging, storage
### 1° – General provisions
The wines are vinified in accordance with local, fair and constant usages.
a) – Analytical standards
Finished wines, ready to be released for consumption within the meaning of Article D. 645-18-I of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code, have a maximum fermentable sugar content (glucose + fructose) of:
- 3 grams per litre;
- or 4 grams per litre if the total acidity is greater than or equal to 55.10 milliequivalents per litre, i.e. 4.13 grams per litre expressed as tartaric acid (or 2.7 grams per litre expressed as H2SO4).
b) – Oenological practices and physical treatments
- The use of pieces of wood is prohibited;
- After enrichment, the wines do not exceed a total alcoholic strength by volume of 13.5 %.
c) – Prohibited equipment
Continuous presses are prohibited.
d) – Vat capacity
Every operator has an overall vat capacity (vinification and storage) equivalent to at least the volume of wine vinified for the previous year’s harvest, as entered either on the harvest declaration, pro rata to the change in the holding’s area in production, or on the production declaration.
e) – Upkeep of the cellar and equipment
The cellar and the equipment are well maintained; this is reflected in particular by:
- general hygiene of the elaboration premises, with general cleanliness, well-maintained floors, adequate drainage and a surface avoiding stagnation;
- separation and specificity of the premises: premises without the same functions must be separated, such as storage areas for plant-protection products, cleaning products or hydrocarbons, from the premises for vinification, ageing and storage of dry goods (corks, cartons);
- absence of hazardous or odorous substances in the vinification, ageing and storage premises (odour).
f) – Ageing
- The wines are aged at least until 15 March of the year following that of the harvest;
- The temperature of the containers during the ageing phase is controlled and less than or equal to 25 °C.
### 2° – Provisions relating to packaging
For every packaged lot, the operator keeps available for the approved control body:
- the information appearing in the register of handling operations referred to in Article D. 645-18 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code;
- the reports of analyses carried out before or after packaging.
These reports are kept for a period of 6 months from the date of packaging.
### 3° – Provisions relating to storage
The operator provides evidence of a protected storage place for wines packaged in bottles.
### 4° – Provisions relating to the circulation of products and placing on the market for the consumer
At the end of the ageing period, the wines are placed on the market for the consumer from 31 March of the year following that of the harvest.
## X. – Link with the geographical area
### 1° – Information on the geographical area
a) – Description of the natural factors contributing to the link
The geographical area is situated at the heart of the plateaux of the “Auxerrois”, in the Paris Basin. It extends over the commune of Chablis in the Yonne department, east of Auxerre. The small town of Chablis is crossed by the Serein, a tributary of the Yonne, on its course through the heart of the “Côte des Bars”.
The cuesta of the “Côte des Bars” is a relief formed of hard limestone, the “Calcaire du Barrois”, forming a cornice above a long slope whose subsoil consists of compact and impermeable marls (clayey limestones), the “Marnes à exogyra virgula”, rich in small comma-shaped oysters that have given their name to the formation. Below the marls, a limestone level forms a small topographic step near the valley floor (“Calcaire à Astartes”). The marls making up the slope are most often masked by a mantle of scree mixing fine materials and coarser limestone elements. The difference in height of the front of the “Côte” reaches 120 metres to 130 metres.
The parcels precisely delimited for the harvesting of the grapes are grouped on a hillside on the right bank of the Serein valley, in the immediate vicinity of the town of Chablis. The complex topography of the hillside, with a general south-westerly orientation, divides it into small slopes whose exposures range from south-east to west. The vine parcels mainly occupy the marly slope with well-drained soils, but locally reach down to the cornice of the “calcaires à astartes”. Altitudes range from 135 metres to 215 metres.
The soils are always calcareous. Lean, very stony and well drained on the limestones at the foot of the slope, they are richer in clay on the marly slope. The mantle of scree covering the impermeable marl, through its variable thickness, brings nuances. It forms a draining layer that warms up well, improving the agronomic conditions.
The “Chablisien” enjoys an oceanic climate slightly modified by continental influences. This climate is characterised by a moderate and regular rainfall regime (only 650 millimetres per year at Auxerre), with no marked summer drought, and rather cool temperatures, with an annual average of 10.8 °C. The climate is characterised by a fairly high risk of winter and spring frosts, which can be catastrophic for the vine.
b) – Description of the human factors contributing to the link
The cultivation of the vine is attested in the “Chablisien” from the early Middle Ages. A monastery was founded in Chablis in 510, then ceded in 867 to the monks of Saint-Martin de Tours fleeing the Viking invasion of the Loire country. It seems that they developed a vineyard close to the village.
In 1114, the Cistercians founded the Abbey of Pontigny near Chablis and gradually developed a vineyard, then set up in the town of Chablis a cellar, the “Petit Pontigny”, whose buildings still exist. Cohabitation between the two abbeys was not without difficulties, but an agreement was finally reached.
The Cistercian wine estate occupied in particular the slope closest to the village, facing south-west, on the right bank of the Serein. This ensemble constitutes the historic core of the vineyard and was to give rise to the “grands crus”.
During the French Revolution, the ecclesiastical vineyards were confiscated and sold at auction. They were then shared among the notables of Chablis, who continued the tradition of quality inherited from past centuries.
Throughout its history, the wine of “Chablis” has remained remarkably stable in its type of dry white wine. In 1186, a gift to the abbey of Pontigny concerned a vine located near Chablis, giving a wine that was “white, and long-keeping”. Nineteenth-century authors also noted this particularity, which made “Chablis” an exception in a wine world dominated by red and “clairet” wines.
Phylloxera, downy mildew and powdery mildew seriously affected the “Chablisien”, which took several decades to recover. Only in the 1950s and 1960s, supported by progress in viticulture, mechanisation and frost protection, was the reconstitution of the vineyard truly relaunched.
The “climats” (customary name, most often the name of a lieu-dit) now recognised under the controlled designation of origin “Chablis grand cru” were cited from the 19th century in wine literature on a par with the best “crus de Bourgogne”. From the beginning of the 20th century they were designated by the producers of the region by the expression “grands crus classés”.
The controlled designation of origin “Chablis grand cru” was recognised by decree in 1938. The grape harvesting area rests precisely on 7 historic lieux-dits situated at the gates of Chablis, whose names were then associated with the name of the controlled designation of origin: “Blanchot”, “Bougros”, “Les Clos”, “Grenouilles”, “Preuses”, “Valmur”, “Vaudésir”.
“Chablis grand cru” comes from a single variety, chardonnay B, known locally as “beaunois”, renowned for producing the great white wines of “Burgundy”. In “Chablis” it reigns unchallenged over the whole vineyard.
The vine is traditionally trained with long pruning. The traditional pruning known as “Chablis pruning” is well suited to the difficult climatic conditions while ensuring regular and moderate production. Single or double Guyot pruning, which became widespread throughout the Burgundian vineyards in the 19th century, is also practised. The development of high-performing plant material through clonal selection favours the development of short pruning allowing good regulation of the vegetation.
The vines are trellised and generally planted along the slope. While this arrangement helps the clay soils to drain, it also causes erosion problems, offset by a return to soil cultivation and grass cover.
Faced with the risk of frost, greater here than in most vineyards, winegrowers have invented methods of protection such as sprinkling and heating the parcels, which require collective organisation and an alert system when the temperature falls dangerously.
The producers of Chablis created their first syndicate in 1908 in order to fight effectively against usurpations of the name “Chablis”. In 2000, some of the producers of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin “Chablis grand cru” joined together in an association, the “Union des Grands crus de Chablis”. In 1923, a cooperative cellar, “La Chablisienne”, was created in Chablis. It still brings together 25 % of the producers of the Chablisien.
In 2010 the vineyard covered an area of 104 hectares, for an average annual production of about 5,000 hectolitres.
### 2° – Information on the quality and characteristics of the products
“Chablis grand cru” is a dry, lively and fruity white wine, characterised by a notable minerality balanced by a fleshy character. Firm in its youth, it gains in aromatic complexity and finesse with age, its acidity and minerality giving it a character of freshness. Its expression across seven “climats” displays a palette of nuances. It is a wine for keeping, reaching its peak at around 10 years.
### 3° – Causal interactions
The controlled designation of origin “Chablis grand cru” owes the lively and mineral character of its exclusively white wines in particular to its cool oceanic climate. This situation is reflected in a strong effect of the mesoclimate and of vintage conditions on the characteristics of the wines.
The geographical area of “Chablis grand cru” enjoys a particularly favourable situation, the exposure and the slopes ensuring optimal sunshine and protection from cold north winds. The site gives the wines great concentration supported by a particular minerality, ensuring their longevity and balance.
The relative diversity of soil and exposure conditions is well reflected in the wines. Fruity and expressive when they come from parcels with the lightest soils, they can be austere in their youth when they come from parcels with the heaviest soils. The expression across 7 “climats” highlights these nuances.
The natural aptitudes of the site have been confirmed over the course of history. Shared between two abbeys from the Middle Ages, then worked by families of Chablis producers, the “climats” have kept over time their image of wines of high quality and great expression. In 1816, Jullien, in his “Topographie de tous les vignobles connus”, cited them in the first class.
Producers have developed specific know-how enabling them to get the best out of the chardonnay B variety in this environment: a pruning system that best regulates the yield and thus ensures optimal ripeness, and protection against frost.
The wines of the controlled designation of origin “Chablis grand cru” are sought after throughout the world. Exports account for 60 % of sales.
## XI. – Transitional measures
### 1° – Training methods
Vine parcels in place before 28 March 2003 which do not comply with the provisions relating to planting density and the spacing between rows set out in the present specification continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are completely grubbed up.
## XII. – Rules of presentation and labelling
### 1° – General provisions
Wines for which, under the terms of the present specification, the controlled designation of origin “Chablis grand cru” is claimed and which are presented under the said appellation may not be declared after the harvest, offered to the public, dispatched, put up for sale or sold unless, in the harvest declaration, in advertisements, on leaflets, labels, invoices and any containers whatsoever, the aforesaid controlled designation of origin is entered.
### 2° – Particular provisions
a) – The name of a climat that may be associated with the controlled designation of origin is placed immediately after the name of the controlled designation of origin and printed in characters whose dimensions, both in height and in width, are not larger than those of the characters making up the name of the controlled designation of origin.
b) – The labelling of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin may specify the larger geographical unit “Vin de Bourgogne” or “Grand Vin de Bourgogne”.
c) – Where the grape variety is indicated on the labelling, this indication does not appear in the same field of vision as the compulsory particulars and is printed in characters whose dimensions do not exceed 2 millimetres.
