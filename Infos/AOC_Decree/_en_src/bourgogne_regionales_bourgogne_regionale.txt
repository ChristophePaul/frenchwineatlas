# CHAPTER I
## I. – Name of the designation
Only wines meeting the specific provisions set out below may claim the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne”, initially recognised by the decree of 31 July 1937.
## II. – Geographical denominations and complementary terms
1° – The name of the controlled designation of origin may be followed by the term “clairet” for wines meeting the production conditions laid down for this term in the present specification.
2° – The name of the controlled designation of origin may be followed by the following complementary geographical denominations, for wines meeting the production conditions laid down for these complementary geographical denominations in the present specification:
- “Chitry”;
- “Côte Chalonnaise”;
- “Côtes d’Auxerre”;
- “Côte d’Or”;
- “Côtes du Couchois”;
- “Côte Saint-Jacques”;
- “Coulanges-la-Vineuse”;
- “Epineuil”;
- “Hautes Côtes de Beaune”;
- “Hautes Côtes de Nuits”;
- “La Chapelle Notre-Dame”;
- “Le Chapitre”;
- “Montrecul” or “Montre-Cul” or “En Montre-Cul”;
- “Tonnerre”.
3° – The name of the controlled designation of origin must be followed by the indication “gamay” for wines from the delimited parcel areas of the controlled designations of origin “Brouilly”, “Chénas”, “Chiroubles”, “Côtes de Brouilly”, “Fleurie”, “Juliénas”, “Morgon”, “Moulin-à-Vent”, “Régnié”, “Saint-Amour” that comply, on the one hand, with the provisions laid down in the respective specifications of those controlled designations of origin and, on the other hand, with the rules on blending of varieties, presentation and labelling laid down in the present specification.

## V. – Grape varieties
### 1° – Grape varieties
a) – The white wines come from the following varieties:
- main varieties: chardonnay B, pinot blanc B;
- accessory variety: pinot gris G.
b) – The red wines come from the following varieties:
- main variety: pinot noir N;
- accessory varieties: chardonnay B, pinot blanc B, pinot gris G and, for the Yonne department only, the césar N variety; the gamay N variety, solely for wines from the delimited parcel areas of the controlled designations of origin “Brouilly”, “Chénas”, “Chiroubles”, “Côtes de Brouilly”, “Fleurie”, “Juliénas”, “Morgon”, “Moulin-à-Vent”, “Régnié”, “Saint-Amour”.
c) – The rosé wines come from the following varieties:
- main varieties: pinot noir N, pinot gris G;
- accessory varieties: pinot blanc B, chardonnay B and, for the Yonne department only, the césar N variety.
### 2° – Proportion rules on the holding
Compliance of the planting is assessed, for the colour concerned, over all the parcels of the holding producing the wine of the controlled designation of origin.
a) – White wines: the proportion of the pinot gris G variety is less than or equal to 30 % of the planting.
b) – Red wines:
- the proportion of the césar N variety is less than or equal to 10 % of the planting;
- the proportion of the gamay N variety is less than or equal to 30 % of the planting;
- the other accessory varieties are authorised only as mixed plantings within the vines; their total proportion is limited to 15 % within each parcel.
c) – Rosé wines:
- the proportion of the césar N variety is less than or equal to 10 % of the planting;
- the other accessory varieties are authorised only as mixed plantings within the vines; their total proportion is limited to 15 % within each parcel.
## VI. – Management of the vineyard
### 1° – Training methods
a) – Planting density
| **GENERAL PROVISIONS** | < |
| The vines have a minimum planting density of 5,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows less than or equal to 2.20 metres. Where the planting density is greater than 8,000 plants per hectare, the vines have a spacing between plants within the same row greater than or equal to 0.50 metre. Where the planting density is less than or equal to 8,000 plants per hectare, the vines have a spacing between plants within the same row greater than or equal to 0.80 metre. Vines planted “en foule” (randomly, without rows) have a minimum planting density of 9,000 plants per hectare and a spacing between plants within the same row greater than 0.50 metre. | < |
| **PARTICULAR PROVISIONS** | < |
| In the delimited vineyard of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” followed by the complementary geographical denominations “Hautes Côtes de Beaune” and “Hautes Côtes de Nuits” | The vines have a minimum planting density of 4,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows less than or equal to 3 metres. |
| Complementary geographical denominations “Chitry”, “Côtes d’Auxerre”, “Côte Chalonnaise”, “Côtes du Couchois”, “Côte Saint-Jacques”, “Coulanges-la-Vineuse”, “Épineuil” and “Tonnerre” | The vines have a minimum planting density of 7,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows less than or equal to 1.30 metres. |
| Complementary geographical denominations “Côte d’Or”, “La Chapelle Notre-Dame”, “Le Chapitre”, “Montrecul” (or “Montre-Cul” or “En Montre-Cul”) | The vines have a minimum planting density of 9,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows less than or equal to 1.25 metres. |

## X. – Link with the geographical area
### 1° – Information on the geographical area
a) – Description of the natural factors contributing to the link
The geographical area rests on the reliefs traditionally devoted to viticulture in the Yonne, Côte-d’Or, Saône-et-Loire and Rhône departments. It thus extends over the territory of more than 300 communes. It is made up of a set of more or less separate vineyards, grouped within “wine-growing Burgundy”, which stretches for about 250 kilometres from north to south. Each of the vineyards is characterised by its own landscapes, geology and climate.
The vineyards of the Yonne and of the “Châtillonnais”, north of the “Côte d’Or”, are planted on the cuestas of the Paris Basin, on sediments dated to the Upper Jurassic, with the exception of the “Vézelien”, planted on Lower and Middle Jurassic formations, and the “Jovinien”, planted on Upper Cretaceous formations. The substrata are mainly marly (clayey limestones), locally calcareous. The vineyard is confined to the best-exposed reliefs of the cuestas, on the flanks of the main valleys draining the region, the Yonne and its tributaries, the Seine and its tributaries. It lies at altitudes of between 150 metres and 300 metres.
From Dijon to the outskirts of Lyon, the vineyards occupy an almost continuous series of linear reliefs. This is the western edge of the Bresse rift, a major tectonic structure that subsided during the Alpine uplift. The substrata are mainly sedimentary, limestones or marls, generally dated to the Jurassic, but also locally to the Triassic. Locally, mainly in Saône-et-Loire, outcrops of Primary-era metamorphic and granitic basement, generating acid soils, bear vines. Although the reliefs of the hinterland are sometimes high (650 metres in Côte-d’Or, 1,000 metres in the “Beaujolais”), the vines are confined to altitudes of between 250 metres and 400 metres.
Each of the vineyards is characterised by its own landscapes, geology and morphology:
- broad valleys cutting wedge-like into the limestone plateaux of the Paris Basin;
- a thin continuous strip lying on the boundary between plain and plateau for “the Côte”;
- a series of parallel reliefs stretching on a north–south axis, the “chaînons” (ridges), in the “Mâconnais”.
“Wine-growing Burgundy” enjoys a rather cool oceanic climate. This climate is characterised by a moderate and regular rainfall regime, with no marked summer drought. Temperatures reflect its coolness, with an annual average of 11 °C. Well marked in the Yonne department, it is somewhat attenuated in the south-east of the geographical area by the screening role played by the relief of the Morvan and Charolais hills, which generates a foehn effect expressed in lower humidity and higher temperatures than the regional reference. The eastern part of the geographical area is affected by continental influences, expressed in relatively low winter temperatures and periods of frost that can be long and intense, but also in autumns that are sometimes very dry and sunny. Southern influences, perceptible above all in the southern part of the geographical area, can momentarily generate high summer temperatures and rising warm sea air, responsible for summer storms.
The northerly situation of the Burgundy vineyard results in a strong effect of mesoclimatic situations on the wine-growing potential, as well as a strong expression of the effect of vintage conditions on the characteristics of the wines. The parcels selected for the harvesting of the grapes correspond to all the sectors traditionally recognised for their aptitude for viticulture. They thus preferentially occupy the well-exposed hillsides of the main reliefs, where the best thermal and water conditions are found.
b) – Description of the human factors contributing to the link
The cultivation of the vine is attested from the Gallo-Roman period (depictions of vines at Escolives-Sainte-Camille near Auxerre, a 1st-century vineyard excavated at Gevrey-Chambertin, the “Speech to Constantine” by Eumenius describing the vineyards of the “Pagus arebrignus” near Beaune in the 4th century). Communication routes such as the Saône (amphorae dated to the mid-1st century AD at Chalon-sur-Saône) or the via Agrippa facilitated the expansion of the vineyard.
In the Middle Ages, the vineyards of “Burgundy” had already acquired economic importance and were known beyond its borders. Auxerre produced the so-called “Basse Bourgogne” wines, while the “Côte d’Or” was known for supplying the “Vins de Beaune”. The role of ecclesiastical structures (abbeys, bishoprics) and of the nobility in building the renown of the wines of “Burgundy” is well known. Cluny, Cîteaux, Pontigny, the Chapter of Langres and the Dukes of Burgundy each contributed in their own way to building a prestigious vineyard and to the influence of its wines. The extent of the Duchy of Burgundy, from Mâcon to Amsterdam, around the 15th century, for example, made it possible to make the wines of “Burgundy” known and to facilitate their trade.
In the 18th century, the significant development of the wine trade generated a new economic power. The wines of “Burgundy” were widely sold to the north of France and of Europe, and even to other continents.
However, “wine-growing Burgundy” became clearly individualised during the 19th century. Old (provinces) or recent (departments) administrative divisions combined with the economic dimension, with urban centres such as Auxerre, Dijon, Beaune, Chalon-sur-Saône or Mâcon ensuring the distribution of the region’s wines. The north of the “Beaujolais” identified itself at that time with the wines of “Mâcon”. The “Tonnerrois” and the “Châtillonnais”, at the gates of Champagne, also turned towards “Burgundy”.
The designation of origin “Bourgogne” was made official by a judgment of the Dijon Court in 1930. The bases proposed by this first judgment have changed very little since, and the current delimitation of the geographical area is inherited from it. The controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” was recognised by decree in 1937. In accordance with this decree, it initially included the wine-growing communes of the Yonne, Côte d’Or and Saône-et-Loire departments, as well as those of the Beaujolais, in the Rhône department.
The geographical area is characterised by a little-diversified planting. For white wines, the chardonnay B variety is dominant, with the local presence of the pinot blanc B variety. For red and rosé wines, the pinot noir N variety is almost exclusive in the three departments of northern Burgundy, the gamay N variety being found in the south of the geographical area. In addition, the pinot gris G variety, known locally as “beurot”, and, in the Yonne, the césar N variety appear anecdotally. These varieties are probably indigenous and closely related (the “pinot” family), selected and adapted to local conditions since the Middle Ages.
The geographical dispersion of the vineyard is accompanied by some differences in production methods. Each small region, inheriting ancient know-how, has thus preserved certain local practices in the training of the vine: arched canes in the “Mâconnais”, Chablis pruning in the Yonne, … Wine landscapes are therefore typical of each place and recognisable. These local identities are expressed through the recognition of complementary geographical denominations corresponding to small regions or communes: “Chitry”, “Côtes d’Auxerre”, “Epineuil”, “Tonnerre”, “Coulanges-la-Vineuse”, “Côte d’Or”, “Hautes-Côtes de Nuits”, “Hautes-Côtes de Beaune”, “Côte chalonnaise”, “Côtes du Couchois”, and sometimes lieux-dits of more limited area: “Côte Saint Jacques”, “Le Chapitre”, “Chapelle Notre Dame” and “Montrecul”.
The vineyard covers about 5,100 hectares, for an annual production of nearly 200,000 hectolitres of red and rosé wines and 80,000 hectolitres of white wines. More than 50 % of the volumes benefit from a complementary geographical denomination, the main volumes being sold under the complementary geographical denominations “Hautes Côtes de Beaune” (40,000 hectolitres, 17 % white wines), “Hautes Côtes de Nuits” (33,000 hectolitres, 22 % white wines) and “Côte châlonnaise” (28,000 hectolitres, 29 % white wines). Owing to their great renown, 40 % to 45 % of the wines of “Bourgogne” are exported, depending on the year.
### 2° – Information on the quality and characteristics of the products
The red wines have a cherry-red colour with deep ruby-red highlights. The nose expresses dominant notes of small fruit accompanied by spicy notes (pepper, liquorice, …) and discreetly woody notes. The palate is structured by fine tannins and an acidity that brings freshness. The aromas, dominated by small fruit, persist long on the palate.
The white wines are limpid and brilliant, with a pale gold to green colour. The nose is marked by fruity notes dominated by citrus, accompanied by white flowers (hawthorn, honeysuckle, acacia). The palate expresses great freshness, with lemony aromas and a persistent, rounder finish with brioche nuances.
The rosé wines show a palette of fairly deep pinks with orange notes. Aromas of small red fruit dominate the nose. On the palate, freshness comes first, with fruity and spicy notes.
The great latitudinal extent of the geographical area is expressed precisely in the wines. In the Yonne the wines are rather structured by a pronounced acidity, which contrasts with the suppler expression of the wines of the southern part of the geographical area.
### 3° – Causal interactions
“Bourgogne” is a northern vineyard where the vine is subject to strong climatic constraint. Planting is therefore confined to the most favourable situations, enjoying mesoclimates that are warmer and drier than the regional climate, and free-draining soils able to remove excess water while offering good fertility and rapid warming. The vineyard is therefore concentrated on the main reliefs, most often calcareous, of moderate altitude.
“Wine-growing Burgundy”, an assembly of small regions with particular identities and profiles giving the regional production a certain diversity reflected in the complementary geographical denominations, is united by important common characteristics:
- a little-diversified and indigenous planting, particularly well suited to the soil and climate conditions;
- the single-variety or almost single-variety character of the wines.
This agronomic unity combines with the diversity of natural conditions and local usages to provide a wide range of white, red and rosé wines sharing the characteristics of northern wines, such as acidity and fruit, giving them undeniable elegance, together with good ageing capacity. The climatic gradient, though discreet, is fully expressed thanks to the pinot noir N and chardonnay B varieties, which are very responsive to variations in the natural environment.
In the northern part of Burgundy (Côtes d’Auxerre, Tonnerre, Châtillonnais, …), the cool oceanic climate is reflected in the wines by a pronounced acidity. In the southern part of Burgundy (Mâconnais), owing to more widely expressed southern influences, the wines are characterised by a suppler expression. Thus, because of its climatic position at the crossroads of the oceanic climate with continental and southern influences, the region is characterised by a well-marked vintage effect.
“Wine-growing Burgundy” as it exists today is the result of a relatively recent collective dynamic. Although all the vineyards that make it up can pride themselves on a prestigious history, they actually united under a common name only in the 19th century. JULLIEN, in 1816, still felt obliged to justify, in his “Topographie de tous les vignobles connus”, his choice to group in a single chapter the wines of “Basse Bourgogne” (Yonne department), those of “Haute Bourgogne” (Côte d’Or and the north of Saône-et-Loire) and finally the “wines of Mâcon”, in which he grouped the “Mâconnais” and part of the “Beaujolais”.
It is likely that the action of the wine trade emerging in the 18th century, as well as the organisation of viticulture at the beginning of the 20th century, were decisive in the emergence of this Burgundian identity. The trade and then the producers themselves took the place of the nobility and the Church, historically the promoters of the “Crus de Bourgogne”, taking charge of the economic dimension of viticulture and ensuring its influence even beyond European borders. This is shown by the work of the Abbé ARNOUX, “Dissertation sur la situation de la Bourgogne, sur les vins qu’elle produit…”, published in London in 1728, originally in French, then in English and German. Thomas JEFFERSON, future President of the United States, visited Burgundy in 1787 and remained, until the end of his life, a great promoter of the Burgundian “crus”, which he helped to make known in America.
The Bourgogne geographical area as it is today (including communes of the Yonne, Côte d’Or, Saône-et-Loire and part of the Rhône departments) is the result of the synthesis of the constraints of the natural environment, from climate to reliefs and soils, with the historical selection of the varieties best suited to express the best of these constraints, and with the construction of a common identity, the fruit of a long history linked to production usages.

- have an external leaf-canopy surface, for the production of one kilogram of grapes, of at least 1.30 square metres for the production of white wines and 1.50 square metres for the production of red and rosé wines.
The volume that may be claimed under the controlled designation of origin is calculated on the basis of the yield authorised for the controlled designation of origin multiplied by a coefficient of 0.80.
d) – Vine parcels in place before the date of approval of the present specification, situated in the delimited vineyard of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” followed by the complementary geographical denominations “Hautes Côtes de Beaune” and “Hautes Côtes de Nuits”, which do not comply with the provisions relating to planting density set out in the present specification and are trained on a “single-plane” trellis, continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up, provided that they:
- have a planting density greater than 3,000 plants per hectare;
- are pruned with a maximum of 6 free eyes per square metre;
- have an external leaf-canopy surface, for the production of one kilogram of grapes, of at least 1.30 square metres for the production of white wines and 1.50 square metres for the production of red and rosé wines.
The volume that may be claimed under the controlled designation of origin is calculated on the basis of the yield authorised for the controlled designation of origin multiplied by a coefficient of 0.90.
e) – Vine parcels in place on 31 July 2009 trained according to the so-called “lyre” system continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up, provided that they:
- have a minimum planting density of 3,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows of less than 3.50 metres and a spacing between plants within the same row of between 0.80 metre and 1 metre;
- are pruned in double Guyot or trained as double Royat cordon; overlapping of canes or cordons is prohibited; each plant bears a maximum of 26 free eyes and the maximum number of free eyes per square metre is 8;
- have a height of trellised foliage of at least 1.20 metres, measured between the lower trellising wire and the upper trimming limit; the trellising consists of two planes that diverge towards the top; the distance between the trellising planes is at least 0.50 metre at the base and between 1 metre and 1.40 metres at the top.
f) – Vine parcels in place on 31 August 1975 and pruned in the so-called “taille à queue du Mâconnais” may be pruned in such a way that, at the phenological stage corresponding to 11 or 12 leaves, each plant bears a maximum of 28 fruit-bearing shoots of the year, including 2 canes each bearing a maximum of 12 fruit-bearing shoots of the year and 2 spurs with 2 free eyes.
## XII. – Rules of presentation and labelling
### 1° – General provisions
Wines for which, under the terms of the present specification, the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” is claimed and which are presented under this appellation may not be declared after the harvest, offered to the public, dispatched, put up for sale or sold unless, in the harvest declaration, in advertisements, on leaflets, labels, invoices and any containers whatsoever, the aforesaid controlled designation of origin is entered.
### 2° – Particular provisions
a) – The complementary geographical denominations “La Chapelle Notre-Dame”, “Le Chapitre”, “Chitry”, “Côtes d’Auxerre”, “Côte d’Or”, “Côte Chalonnaise”, “Côtes du Couchois”, “Côte Saint-Jacques”, “Coulanges-la-Vineuse”, “Epineuil”, “Hautes Côtes de Beaune”, “Hautes Côtes de Nuits”, “Montrecul” (or “Montre-Cul” or “En Montre-Cul”) and “Tonnerre” are written after the name of the controlled designation of origin or immediately below it, and printed in characters whose dimensions, both in height and in width, do not exceed those of the characters of the name of the controlled designation of origin.
b) – Where the name of the controlled designation of origin is supplemented by the indication of the main variety, this indication is written immediately below and printed in characters whose dimensions, both in height and in width, do not exceed two thirds of those of the characters of the name of the controlled designation of origin.
c) – The indication “gamay” is written immediately below the name of the controlled designation of origin in characters whose dimensions, both in height and in width, are equal to those of the characters of the name of the controlled designation of origin.
d) – The labelling of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin may specify the name of a smaller geographical unit, provided that:
- it is the name of a registered lieu-dit (cadastral locality);
- it appears on the harvest declaration.
The name of the registered lieu-dit is printed in characters whose dimensions, both in height and in width, are not larger than half those of the characters making up the name of the controlled designation of origin.
e) – The labelling of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin may specify the larger geographical unit “Vin de Bourgogne”.
# CHAPTER II
## I. – Declaration obligations
#### 1. Prior declaration of parcel assignment
If the operator wishes to assign to the production of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” parcels capable of producing wines of a controlled designation of origin whose vineyard production conditions are more restrictive, it declares the list of these parcels to the defence and management body before 15 May preceding the harvest. This declaration specifies in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- for each parcel: the cadastral reference, the area, the grape variety and the planting density.
#### 2. Declaration of renunciation to produce
Before 15 June preceding the harvest, the operator declares to the defence and management body the parcels for which it renounces producing the controlled designation of origin. The defence and management body transmits this declaration as soon as possible to the approved control body.
#### 3. Claim declaration
The claim declaration is sent to the defence and management body at least fifteen days before circulation between authorised warehousekeepers and at the latest on 10 December of the year of harvest.
It indicates in particular:
- the designation claimed;
- the volume of the wine;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the name and address of the applicant;
- the place of storage of the wine.
It is accompanied in particular by a copy of the harvest declaration and, as the case may be, a copy of the production declaration or an extract of the stock accounts for buyers of grapes and musts.
#### 4. Declaration prior to transaction and collections
Every operator wishing to sell in bulk a wine benefiting from the controlled designation of origin makes a transaction declaration for the lot concerned to the approved control body within time limits set in the control plan of between six and fifteen working days before any collection.
This declaration, accompanied where applicable by a copy of the purchase contract, specifies in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the identification of the lot;
- the volume of the lot;
- the identification of the containers;
- the identity of the buyer.
If collections are made for volumes smaller than those set out in the transaction declaration, the operator informs the approved control body in writing.
#### 5. Declaration of release for consumption
Every operator declares to the approved control body each lot of wine intended to be released for consumption within the meaning of Article D. 645-18-I of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code.
This declaration may also be drawn up for lots already packaged. It is made within time limits set in the control plan of between six and fifteen working days before release for consumption or before the lots concerned are dispatched from the operator’s cellars.
It specifies in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the identification of the lot;
- the volume of the lot;
- the lot number for wines already packaged;
- the identification of the containers for non-packaged wines.
However, making available to the approved control body the register referred to in Article D. 645-18 II of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code serves as the declaration of release for consumption, under the conditions set out in the control plan.
#### 6. Declaration relating to the dispatch outside the national territory of a non-packaged wine
Every operator wishing to dispatch outside the national territory a non-packaged wine benefiting from the controlled designation of origin declares it to the approved control body within time limits set in the control plan of between six and fifteen working days before any dispatch.
#### 7. Fallback declaration
Every operator carrying out a fallback of a wine benefiting from the controlled designation of origin into a more general controlled designation of origin sends the defence and management body and the approved control body a monthly declaration at the latest on the 1st working day following the tenth day of each month.
This declaration indicates in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the controlled designation of origin under which the wine was claimed;
- the more general controlled designation of origin of the fallback;
- the volume concerned by the fallback;
- the vintage;
- the state of the lot concerned (bulk, bottle, hermetically sealed vacuum containers…).
The approved control body transmits without delay a copy of the monthly declaration to the approved control body for the more general controlled designation of origin concerned.
The defence and management body transmits without delay to the defence and management body of the more general controlled designation of origin concerned the list of operators who have made a monthly fallback declaration, together with a summary statement indicating:
- the more general controlled designation of origin of the fallback;
- the volume concerned by the fallback;
- the vintage(s);
- the state of the lot(s) concerned (bulk, bottle, hermetically sealed vacuum containers…).
#### 8. Downgrading declaration
Every operator carrying out a downgrading of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin sends the defence and management body and the approved control body a monthly declaration within time limits set in the control plan. This declaration indicates in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the volume downgraded;
- the vintage;
- the date of the downgrading.
#### 9. Declaration of equipment for TSE
Every operator holding a concentration apparatus declares it upon purchase to the defence and management body, specifying its specifications. The defence and management body keeps an up-to-date list of operators holding such equipment and sends it each year to the services of the National Institute of Origin and Quality at the latest on 1 September.
Every operator using a service provider declares it to the defence and management body, which draws up the list of these operators and sends it each year to the services of the National Institute of Origin and Quality at the latest on 1 September.
#### 10. Reworking of parcels
Before any development or any works liable to modify the morphology, the subsoil, the arable layer (including any addition of exogenous soil) or the elements that guarantee the integrity and sustainability of the soils of a parcel intended for the production of the controlled designation of origin, with the exception of classic deep-ploughing works, the operator sends a declaration to the defence and management body at least four weeks before the planned start date of the works.
The defence and management body transmits, without delay, a copy of this declaration to the services of the National Institute of Origin and Quality.
## II. – Keeping of registers
#### 1. General plan of the storage and vinification places
Every vinifying operator keeps up to date and available for the approved control body a general plan of the storage and vinification places, allowing in particular the number, designation and capacity of the containers to be identified.
#### 2. TSE register
Every operator carrying out partial concentration of musts keeps an up-to-date TSE register including in particular:
- the initial volume;
- the volume of water evaporated;
- the identification of the lot after concentration (volume and potential alcoholic strength).
