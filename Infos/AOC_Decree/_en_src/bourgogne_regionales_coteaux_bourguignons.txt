# CHAPTER I
## I. – Name of the designation
Only wines meeting the specific provisions set out below may claim the controlled designation of origin “Coteaux Bourguignons”, initially recognised by the decree of 31 July 1937 under the name “Bourgogne grand ordinaire” or “Bourgogne ordinaire”. The following denominations, “Bourgogne grand ordinaire” and “Bourgogne ordinaire”, are traditionally used in place of the name of the controlled designation of origin.
## II. – Geographical denominations and complementary terms
1° – The name of the controlled designation of origin may be followed by the term “clairet” for wines meeting the production conditions laid down for this term in the present specification.
2° – The name of the controlled designation of origin may be followed or supplemented by the term “primeur” or “nouveau” for wines meeting the production conditions laid down for this term in the present specification.
## III. – Colour and types of product
1° – The controlled designation of origin “Coteaux Bourguignons” is reserved for still white, red or rosé wines.
2° – The term “clairet” is reserved for still rosé wines.
3° – The term “primeur” or “nouveau” is reserved for still white wines.

## V. – Grape varieties
### 1° – Grape varieties
a) – The white wines come from the following varieties: aligoté B, chardonnay B, melon B, pinot blanc B and pinot gris G.
b) – The red wines come from the following varieties:
- main varieties: gamay N, pinot noir N and, for the Yonne department only, the césar N variety;
- accessory varieties: aligoté B, chardonnay B, gamay de Bouze N, gamay de Chaudenay N, melon B, pinot blanc B, pinot gris G.
c) – The rosé wines come from the following varieties:
- main varieties: gamay N, pinot gris G, pinot noir N and, for the Yonne department only, the césar N variety;
- accessory varieties: aligoté B, chardonnay B, melon B and pinot blanc B.
### 2° – Proportion rules on the holding
Compliance of the planting is assessed, for the colour concerned, over all the parcels of the holding producing the wine of the controlled designation of origin.
a) – Red wines:
- the proportion of the accessory varieties, together or separately, is less than or equal to 10 % of the planting;
- the accessory varieties chardonnay B, pinot blanc B and pinot gris G are also authorised as mixed plantings within the vines; their total proportion is limited to 15 % within each parcel.
b) – Rosé wines:
- the proportion of the accessory varieties, together or separately, is less than or equal to 10 % of the planting;
- the accessory varieties chardonnay B and pinot blanc B are also authorised as mixed plantings within the vines; their total proportion is limited to 15 % within each parcel.
## VI. – Management of the vineyard
### 1° – Training methods
a) – Planting density
- The vines have a minimum planting density of 5,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows less than or equal to 2.50 metres;
- In the delimited vineyard of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” followed by the complementary geographical denominations “Hautes Côtes de Beaune” and “Hautes Côtes de Nuits”, the vines have a minimum planting density of 4,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows less than or equal to 3 metres;
- Where the planting density is greater than 8,000 plants per hectare, the vines have a spacing between plants within the same row greater than or equal to 0.50 metre;
- Where the planting density is less than or equal to 8,000 plants per hectare, the vines have a spacing between plants within the same row greater than or equal to 0.80 metre.
Vines planted “en foule” (randomly, without rows) have a minimum planting density of 9,000 plants per hectare and a spacing between plants greater than 0.50 metre. Vines trained according to the so-called “lyre” system have a minimum planting density of 3,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows of less than 3.50 metres and a spacing between plants within the same row of between 0.80 metre and 1 metre.

The average maximum load per parcel is set at:
- 12,000 kilograms per hectare for white wines;
- 10,000 kilograms per hectare for red and rosé wines.
e) – Threshold of missing vines

f) – Cultivation condition of the vine
The parcels are managed so as to ensure a good overall cultivation condition of the vine, in particular its sanitary condition and the upkeep of its soil.
### 2° – Other cultivation practices
a) – In order to preserve the characteristics of the physical and biological environment, which constitutes a fundamental element of the terroir:
- Permanent grass cover of the headlands is compulsory;
- Any substantial modification of the morphology, the subsoil, the arable layer or the elements that guarantee the integrity and sustainability of the soils of a parcel intended for the production of the controlled designation of origin is prohibited, with the exception of classic deep-ploughing works.
b) – Vine plantings and replacements are carried out with healthy plant material that has undergone hot-water treatment.
### 3° – Irrigation
Irrigation is prohibited.

### 2° – Entry into production of young vines
The benefit of the controlled designation of origin may only be granted to wines coming from:
- parcels of young vines only from the 2nd year following that in the course of which planting was carried out in place before 31 July;
- parcels of young vines only from the 1st year following that in the course of which grafting in place was carried out before 31 July;
- parcels of vines that have been top-grafted, at the earliest in the 1st year following that in the course of which the top-grafting was carried out before 31 July, and once the parcels no longer contain any varieties other than those permitted for the appellation. By derogation, in the year following that in the course of which the top-grafting was carried out before 31 July, the varieties permitted for the appellation may represent only 80 % of the planting of each parcel concerned.
## IX. – Processing, elaboration, ageing, packaging, storage
### 1° – General provisions
The wines are vinified in accordance with local, fair and constant usages.
a) – Blending of varieties
- The proportion of the accessory varieties, together or separately, is less than or equal to 10 % of the blend;
- Red and rosé wines produced from parcels with mixed plantings are vinified by blending the grapes.
b) – Malolactic fermentation
At the packaging stage, red wines have a maximum malic acid content of 0.4 gram per litre.
c) – Analytical standards
Finished wines, ready to be released for consumption within the meaning of Article D. 645-18-I of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code, have a maximum fermentable sugar content (glucose + fructose) of:
| COLOUR OF THE WINES | MAXIMUM FERMENTABLE SUGAR CONTENT (glucose + fructose) |
| White wines | – 3 grams per litre; – or 4 grams per litre if the total acidity is greater than or equal to 55.10 milliequivalents per litre, i.e. 4.13 grams per litre expressed as tartaric acid (or 2.7 grams per litre expressed as H2SO4). |
| Rosé wines | 3 grams per litre |
| Red wines | 2 grams per litre |
d) – Oenological practices and physical treatments
- Subtractive enrichment techniques (TSE) are authorised for red wines within the limit of a concentration rate of 10 %;
- For the production of rosé wines, the use of oenological charcoal, alone or mixed in preparations, is prohibited;
- After enrichment, the wines do not exceed a total alcoholic strength by volume of 12.5 % for white wines and 13 % for red and rosé wines.
e) – Prohibited equipment
Continuous presses are prohibited.
f) – Vat capacity
Every operator has an overall vat capacity (vinification and storage) equivalent to at least:
- for white and rosé wines, the volume of wine vinified for the previous year’s harvest, as entered either on the harvest declaration, pro rata to the change in the holding’s area in production, or on the production declaration;
- for red wines, 150 % of the volume of wine vinified for the previous year’s harvest, as entered either on the harvest declaration, pro rata to the change in the holding’s area in production, or on the production declaration.
g) – Upkeep of the cellar and equipment
The cellar and the equipment are well maintained; this is reflected in particular by:
- general hygiene of the elaboration premises, with general cleanliness, well-maintained floors, adequate drainage and a surface avoiding stagnation;
- harmlessness of the equipment and products coming into contact with the wine;
- separation and specificity of the premises: premises without the same functions must be separated, such as storage areas for plant-protection products, cleaning products or hydrocarbons, from the premises for vinification, ageing and storage of dry goods (corks, cartons);
- management of wine effluents: effluents must be removed as quickly as possible from foodstuff premises and deposited in well-maintained containers that are easy to clean and can be closed; waste storage areas must be kept clean; waste must be disposed of hygienically and in an environmentally friendly manner; a waste storage and removal area must be provided;
- absence of hazardous or odorous substances in the vinification, ageing and storage premises (odour).
h) – Ageing
The temperature of the containers during the ageing phase is controlled and less than or equal to 25 °C.
### 2° – Provisions relating to packaging
For every packaged lot, the operator keeps available for the approved control body:
- the information appearing in the register of handling operations referred to in Article D. 645-18 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code;
- the reports of analyses carried out before or after packaging.
These reports are kept for a period of 6 months from the date of packaging.
### 3° – Provisions relating to storage
The operator provides evidence of a protected storage place for wines packaged in unlabelled bottles, with a temperature of between 5 °C and 22 °C.
### 4° – Provisions relating to the circulation of products and placing on the market for the consumer
a) – Date of placing on the market for the consumer
The wines are placed on the market for the consumer in accordance with the provisions of Article D. 645-17 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code.
b) – Period during which the wines may not circulate between authorised warehousekeepers
Wines eligible for the term “primeur” or “nouveau” may only circulate between authorised warehousekeepers from the 38th day preceding the 3rd Thursday of November of the year of harvest.
## X. – Link with the geographical area
### 1° – Information on the geographical area
a) – Description of the natural factors contributing to the link
The geographical area rests on the reliefs traditionally devoted to viticulture in the Yonne, Côte-d’Or, Saône-et-Loire and Rhône departments. It groups together a more or less continuous set of vineyards stretching for about 250 kilometres from north to south. It thus extends over the territory of more than 300 communes. Each of the vineyards is characterised by its own landscapes, geology and climate.
The vineyards of the Yonne and of the “Châtillonnais”, north of the “Côte d’Or”, are planted on the cuestas of the Paris Basin, on sediments dated to the Upper Jurassic, with the exception of the “Vézelien”, planted on Lower and Middle Jurassic formations, and the “Jovinien”, planted on Upper Cretaceous formations. The substrata are mainly marly (clayey limestones), locally calcareous. The vineyard is confined to the best-exposed reliefs of the cuestas, on the flanks of the main valleys draining the region, the Yonne and its tributaries, the Seine and its tributaries. It lies at altitudes of between 150 metres and 300 metres.
From Dijon to the outskirts of Lyon, the vineyards occupy an almost continuous series of linear reliefs. This is the western edge of the Bresse rift, a major tectonic structure that subsided during the Alpine uplift. The substrata are mainly sedimentary, limestones or marls, generally dated to the Jurassic, but also locally to the Triassic. Locally, mainly in Saône-et-Loire, outcrops of Primary-era metamorphic and granitic basement, generating acid soils, bear vines. Although the reliefs of the hinterland are sometimes high (650 metres in Côte-d’Or, 1,000 metres in the “Beaujolais”), the vines are confined to altitudes of between 250 metres and 400 metres.
Each of the vineyards is characterised by its own landscapes, geology and morphology:
- broad valleys cutting wedge-like into the limestone plateaux of the Paris Basin;
- a thin continuous strip lying on the boundary between plain and plateau for “the Côte”;
- a series of parallel reliefs stretching on a north–south axis, the “chaînons” (ridges), in the “Mâconnais”.
“Wine-growing Burgundy” enjoys a rather cool oceanic climate. This climate is characterised by a moderate and regular rainfall regime, with no marked summer drought. Temperatures reflect its coolness, with an annual average of 11 °C. Well marked in the Yonne department, it is somewhat attenuated in the south-east of the geographical area by the screening role played by the relief of the Morvan and Charolais hills, which generates a foehn effect expressed in lower humidity and higher temperatures than the regional reference. The eastern part of the geographical area is affected by continental influences, expressed in relatively low winter temperatures and periods of frost that can be long and intense, but also in autumns that are sometimes very dry and sunny. Southern influences, perceptible above all in the southern part of the geographical area, can momentarily generate high summer temperatures and rising warm sea air, responsible for summer storms.
The parcels delimited for the harvesting of the grapes correspond to all the sectors traditionally recognised for their aptitude for viticulture. They thus preferentially occupy the well-exposed hillsides of the main reliefs, as well as the piedmonts, the cool plateaux and the back slopes facing north and west, all situations ensuring optimal drainage and good soil-warming capacity.
b) – Description of the human factors contributing to the link
The cultivation of the vine is attested from the Gallo-Roman period. In the Middle Ages, the vineyards of “Burgundy” had already acquired economic importance and were known beyond its borders. The role of ecclesiastical structures (abbeys, bishoprics) and of the nobility in building the renown of the wines of “Burgundy” is well known. Cluny, Cîteaux, Pontigny, the Chapters of Langres and Autun and the Dukes of Burgundy each contributed in their own way to building a prestigious vineyard and to the influence of its wines.
In the 18th century, the significant development of the wine trade generated a new economic power. The wines of “Burgundy” were widely sold to the north of France and of Europe, and even to other continents. However, “wine-growing Burgundy” became clearly individualised during the 19th century. Old (provinces) or recent (departments) administrative divisions combined with the economic dimension, with urban centres such as Auxerre, Dijon, Beaune, Chalon-sur-Saône or Mâcon ensuring the distribution of the region’s wines. The north of the “Beaujolais” identified itself at that time with the wines of “Mâcon”. The “Tonnerrois” and the “Châtillonnais”, at the gates of Champagne, also turned towards “Burgundy”.
Wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin “Coteaux Bourguignons” originate from the traditional and large production of so-called “grands ordinaires” or “ordinaires” wines, sometimes also called “vins de gamets”, thus designating their main nature. The economic importance of these “grands ordinaires” or “ordinaires” wines was considerable from the 19th century. They supplied local consumption as well as that of nearby towns and industrial centres, such as Le Creusot or the mining basins of Autun, Epinac or Montceau.
In the 19th century and at the beginning of the 20th century, the so-called “ordinaires” wines, produced from common varieties such as gamay N or melon B, were distinguished from the so-called “grands ordinaires” or “Passe-tout-grains” wines, produced from a larger proportion of “fine varieties” such as pinot noir N or chardonnay B. At that time, these notions were used above all in Côte-d’Or, to distinguish this production from the prestigious “crus” of “the Côte”.
Between 1919 and 1930, various judgments gradually regulated the designation of origin “Bourgogne ordinaire”, assimilating it to the designation of origin “Bourgogne grand ordinaire” and extending it to the whole of “wine-growing Burgundy”. The controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne ordinaire” or “Bourgogne grand ordinaire” was recognised by decree in 1937. This production is now designated under the name “Coteaux Bourguignons”.
The planting is relatively little diversified, made up of the traditional Burgundian varieties. For white wines, the chardonnay B and aligoté B varieties dominate, with the local presence of the pinot blanc B and melon B varieties. For red and rosé wines, the gamay N variety is the majority variety, accompanied by the pinot noir N and pinot gris G varieties, the latter known locally as “beurot”. In the Yonne, the césar N and tressot N varieties appear anecdotally.
### 2° – Information on the quality and characteristics of the products
The red and rosé wines are very fruity, with a fairly fine tannic structure, and can express great finesse. The white wines are generally floral and fresh. These characteristics are preserved by a short ageing. They are wines to be drunk young in order to enjoy their full aromatic power.
### 3° – Causal interactions
“Bourgogne” is a northern vineyard where the vine is subject to strong climatic constraint. Planting is therefore confined to the most favourable situations, characterised by reliefs enjoying both mesoclimates that are warmer and drier than the regional climate and free-draining soils able to remove excess water while offering good fertility and rapid warming.
Within these privileged sites, the parcels delimited for the harvesting of the grapes intended for the production of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin “Coteaux Bourguignons” occupy all situations and extend to sites suited to preserving a certain freshness, in particular piedmonts with relatively heavy soils, or plateaux and back slopes with a cool, well-ventilated climate.
With the exception of the césar N variety, about which little is known, all the other varieties are probably indigenous and closely related (the “pinot” family), selected and adapted to local conditions since the Middle Ages. The planting, particularly well suited to the soil and climate conditions of the geographical area, combines with the diversity of natural conditions to provide a wide range of white, red and rosé wines sharing the characteristics of northern wines, such as freshness and fruit, giving them undeniable elegance.
“Coteaux Bourguignons” appears as an important component of Burgundian wine production. These are wines to be drunk young, in order to appreciate their fruity identity, not intended for keeping but expressing characteristics specific to the region, reflecting at once the climate, the indigenous planting and local know-how.
## XI. – Transitional measures
### 1° – Grape varieties
a) – For the production of red wines, vine parcels in place on 31 July 2009 planted with the tressot N variety continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up.
b) – For the production of white wines, vine parcels in place on 31 July 2009 planted with the sacy B variety continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up.
### 2° – Training methods
a) – Vine parcels in place on 31 July 2009, situated in the communes of the Rhône and Saône-et-Loire departments included in the geographical area of the controlled designation of origin “Beaujolais”, with a planting density of between 4,000 and 5,000 plants per hectare, continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up.
b) – Vine parcels in place on 31 July 2009, situated in the Yonne department, with a planting density of less than 5,000 plants per hectare continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up.
c) – Vine parcels in place before 1980, situated outside the delimited vineyard of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” followed by the complementary geographical denominations “Hautes Côtes de Beaune” and “Hautes Côtes de Nuits”, which do not comply with the provisions relating to planting density set out in the present specification and are trained on a “single-plane” trellis, continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up, provided that they:
- have a planting density greater than 3,000 plants per hectare;
- are pruned with a maximum of 6 free eyes per square metre;
- have an external leaf-canopy surface, for the production of one kilogram of grapes, of at least 1.30 square metres for the production of white wines and 1.50 square metres for the production of red and rosé wines.
The volume that may be claimed under the controlled designation of origin is calculated on the basis of the yield authorised for the controlled designation of origin multiplied by a coefficient of 0.80.
d) – Vine parcels in place before the date of approval of the present specification, situated in the delimited vineyard of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” followed by the complementary geographical denominations “Hautes Côtes de Beaune” and “Hautes Côtes de Nuits”, which do not comply with the provisions relating to planting density set out in the present specification and are trained on a “single-plane” trellis, continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up, provided that they:
- have a planting density greater than 3,000 plants per hectare;
- are pruned with a maximum of 6 free eyes per square metre;
- have an external leaf-canopy surface, for the production of one kilogram of grapes, of at least 1.30 square metres for the production of white wines and 1.50 square metres for the production of red and rosé wines.
e) – As a transitional measure, vine parcels in place before 31 August 1975 and pruned in the so-called “taille à queue du Mâconnais” may be pruned in such a way that, at the phenological stage corresponding to 11 or 12 leaves, each plant bears a maximum of 28 fruit-bearing shoots of the year, including 2 canes each bearing a maximum of 12 fruit-bearing shoots of the year and 2 spurs with 2 free eyes.
### 3° – Labelling
Wines meeting the provisions of the present specification may be marketed under the traditionally used denominations “Bourgogne grand ordinaire” or “Bourgogne ordinaire”, referred to in chapter I, I, second paragraph, in place of the name of the controlled designation of origin, at the latest until the 5th year following the date of registration of the denomination “Coteaux Bourguignons” in e-Bacchus.
## XII. – Rules of presentation and labelling
### 1° – General provisions
Wines for which, under the terms of the present specification, the controlled designation of origin “Coteaux Bourguignons” is claimed and which are presented under the said appellation may not be declared after the harvest, offered to the public, dispatched, put up for sale or sold unless, in the harvest declaration, in advertisements, on leaflets, labels, invoices and any containers whatsoever, the aforesaid controlled designation of origin is entered.
### 2° – Particular provisions
a) – Wines benefiting from the term “primeur” or “nouveau” must be presented with the indication of the vintage, which appears on the label bearing all the compulsory particulars.
b) – The labelling of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin may specify the name of a smaller geographical unit, provided that:
- it is the name of a registered lieu-dit (cadastral locality);
- it appears on the harvest declaration.
The name of the registered lieu-dit is printed in characters whose dimensions, both in height and in width, are not larger than half those of the characters making up the name of the controlled designation of origin.
# CHAPTER II
## I. – Declaration obligations
#### 1. Prior declaration of parcel assignment
If the operator wishes to assign to the production of the controlled designation of origin “Coteaux Bourguignons” parcels capable of producing wines of a more restrictive controlled designation of origin, it declares the list of these parcels to the defence and management body before 15 May preceding the harvest. This declaration specifies in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- for each parcel: the cadastral reference, the area, the grape variety and the planting density.
#### 2. Declaration of renunciation to produce
Before 15 June preceding the harvest, the operator declares to the defence and management body the parcels for which it renounces producing the controlled designation of origin. The defence and management body transmits this declaration as soon as possible to the approved control body.
#### 3. Claim declaration
The claim declaration is sent to the defence and management body at least fifteen days before circulation between authorised warehousekeepers and at the latest on 10 December of the year of harvest.
It indicates in particular:
- the designation claimed;
- the volume of the wine;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the name and address of the applicant;
- the place of storage of the wine.
It is accompanied in particular by a copy of the harvest declaration and, as the case may be, a copy of the production declaration or an extract of the stock accounts for buyers of grapes and musts.
#### 4. Declaration prior to transaction and collections
Every operator wishing to sell in bulk a wine benefiting from the controlled designation of origin makes a transaction declaration for the lot concerned to the approved control body within time limits set in the control plan of between six and fifteen working days before any collection.
This declaration, accompanied where applicable by a copy of the purchase contract, specifies in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the identification of the lot;
- the volume of the lot;
- the identification of the containers;
- the identity of the buyer.
If collections are made for volumes smaller than those set out in the transaction declaration, the operator informs the approved control body in writing.
#### 5. Declaration of release for consumption
Every operator declares to the approved control body each lot of wine intended to be released for consumption within the meaning of Article D. 645-18-I of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code.
This declaration may also be drawn up for lots already packaged. It is made within time limits set in the control plan of between six and fifteen working days before release for consumption or before the lots concerned are dispatched from the operator’s cellars.
It specifies in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the identification of the lot;
- the volume of the lot;
- the lot number for wines already packaged;
- the identification of the containers for non-packaged wines.
However, making available to the approved control body the register referred to in Article D. 645-18 II of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code serves as the declaration of release for consumption, under the conditions set out in the control plan.
#### 6. Declaration relating to the dispatch outside the national territory of a non-packaged wine
Every operator wishing to dispatch outside the national territory a non-packaged wine benefiting from the controlled designation of origin declares it to the approved control body within time limits set in the control plan of between six and fifteen working days before any dispatch.
#### 7. Downgrading declaration
Every operator carrying out a downgrading of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin sends the defence and management body and the approved control body a monthly declaration within time limits set in the control plan. This declaration indicates in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the volume downgraded;
- the vintage;
- the date of the downgrading.
#### 8. Declaration of equipment for TSE
Every operator holding a concentration apparatus declares it upon purchase to the defence and management body, specifying its specifications. The defence and management body keeps an up-to-date list of operators holding such equipment and sends it each year to the services of the National Institute of Origin and Quality at the latest on 1 September.
Every operator using a service provider declares it to the defence and management body, which draws up the list of these operators and sends it each year to the services of the National Institute of Origin and Quality at the latest on 1 September.
#### 9. Reworking of parcels
Before any development or any works liable to modify the morphology, the subsoil, the arable layer (including any addition of exogenous soil) or the elements that guarantee the integrity and sustainability of the soils of a parcel intended for the production of the controlled designation of origin, with the exception of classic deep-ploughing works, the operator sends a declaration to the defence and management body at least four weeks before the planned start date of the works.
The defence and management body transmits, without delay, a copy of this declaration to the services of the National Institute of Origin and Quality.
## II. – Keeping of registers
#### 1. General plan of the storage and vinification places
Every vinifying operator keeps up to date and available for the approved control body a general plan of the storage and vinification places, allowing in particular the number, designation and capacity of the containers to be identified.
#### 2. TSE register
Every operator carrying out partial concentration of musts keeps an up-to-date TSE register including in particular:
- the initial volume;
- the volume of water evaporated;
- the identification of the lot after concentration (volume and potential alcoholic strength).
