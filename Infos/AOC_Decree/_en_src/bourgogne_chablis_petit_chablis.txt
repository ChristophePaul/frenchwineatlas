# CHAPTER I
## I. – Name of the designation
Only wines meeting the specific provisions set out below may claim the controlled designation of origin “Petit Chablis”, initially recognised by the decree of 5 January 1944.
## II. – Geographical denominations and complementary terms
No particular provision.
## III. – Colour and types of product
The controlled designation of origin “Petit Chablis” is reserved for still white wines.
## IV. – Areas and zones in which the various operations are carried out

## V. – Grape varieties
The wines come exclusively from the chardonnay B variety.
## VI. – Management of the vineyard
### 1° – Training methods
a) – Planting density
- The vines have a minimum planting density of 5,500 plants per hectare, with an average spacing between rows less than or equal to 1.20 metres, except for vines planted on slopes of 40 % or more, for which this spacing is less than or equal to 1.60 metres;
- Where the spacing is irregular, the spacing between rows does not exceed 2 metres;
- The vines have a spacing between plants within the same row greater than or equal to 0.80 metre.
b) – Pruning rules
The wines come from vines pruned according to the following provisions:
| GENERAL PROVISIONS |
| The vines are pruned with a maximum of 14 free eyes per plant and a maximum of 10 free eyes per square metre: – either short pruning (vines trained as Royat cordon); – or long pruning (single and double Guyot pruning, and Chablis pruning). |
| **PARTICULAR PROVISION** |
| Whatever the pruning method, the vines may be pruned with additional free eyes provided that, at the phenological stage corresponding to 11 or 12 leaves, the number of fruit-bearing shoots of the year per plant is less than or equal to the number of free eyes defined in the pruning rules. |
c) – Trellising and foliage height rules
- The height of trellised foliage is at least equal to 0.6 times the average spacing between rows, the height of trellised foliage being measured between the lower limit of the foliage, set at least 0.30 metre above the ground, and the upper trimming limit;
- The vines must be trellised and the trellising is maintained.
d) – Average maximum load per parcel
The average maximum load per parcel is set at 10,500 kilograms per hectare.
e) – Threshold of missing vines
The percentage of dead or missing vine plants referred to in Article D. 645-4 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code is set at 20 %.
f) – Cultivation condition of the vine
The parcels are managed so as to ensure a good overall cultivation condition of the vine, reflected in particular by:
- control of a good sanitary condition, making it possible to obtain healthy foliage and healthy berries;
- upkeep of the soil, namely control of grass cover by a grass height of less than half the trellising height (height between the ground and the upper trellising wire), and control of erosion through the absence of visible roots.
### 2° – Other cultivation practices
a) – In order to preserve the characteristics of the physical and biological environment, which constitutes a fundamental element of the terroir:
- Permanent grass cover of the headlands and banks is compulsory; destruction of the plant cover is tolerated occasionally during works to bring soil back up the slope;
- Any substantial modification of the morphology, the subsoil, the arable layer or the elements that guarantee the integrity and sustainability of the soils of a parcel intended for the production of the controlled designation of origin is prohibited, with the exception of classic deep-ploughing works.
b) – Vine plantings and replacements are carried out with healthy plant material that has undergone hot-water treatment or any other method making it possible to combat flavescence dorée.
### 3° – Irrigation
Irrigation is prohibited.

### 2° – Entry into production of young vines
The benefit of the controlled designation of origin may only be granted to wines coming from:
- parcels of young vines only from the 2nd year following that in the course of which planting was carried out in place before 31 July;
- parcels of young vines only from the 1st year following that in the course of which grafting in place was carried out before 31 July;
- parcels of vines that have been top-grafted, at the earliest in the 1st year following that in the course of which the top-grafting was carried out before 31 July, and once the parcels no longer contain any variety other than that permitted for the appellation.
## IX. – Processing, elaboration, ageing, packaging, storage
### 1° – General provisions
The wines are vinified in accordance with local, fair and constant usages.
a) – Analytical standards
Finished wines, ready to be released for consumption within the meaning of Article D. 645-18-I of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code, have a maximum fermentable sugar content (glucose + fructose) of:
- 3 grams per litre;
- or 4 grams per litre if the total acidity is greater than or equal to 55.10 milliequivalents per litre, i.e. 4.13 grams per litre expressed as tartaric acid (or 2.7 grams per litre expressed as H2SO4).
b) – Oenological practices and physical treatments
- The use of pieces of wood is prohibited;
- After enrichment, the wines do not exceed a total alcoholic strength by volume of 12.5 %.
c) – Prohibited equipment
Continuous presses are prohibited.
d) – Vat capacity
Every operator has an overall vat capacity (vinification and storage) equivalent to at least the volume of wine vinified for the previous year’s harvest, as entered either on the harvest declaration, pro rata to the change in the holding’s area in production, or on the production declaration.
e) – Upkeep of the cellar and equipment
The cellar and the equipment are well maintained; this is reflected in particular by:
- general hygiene of the elaboration premises, with general cleanliness, well-maintained floors, adequate drainage and a surface avoiding stagnation;
- separation and specificity of the premises: premises without the same functions must be separated, such as storage areas for plant-protection products, cleaning products or hydrocarbons, from the premises for vinification, ageing and storage of dry goods (corks, cartons);
- absence of hazardous or odorous substances in the vinification, ageing and storage premises (odour).
f) – Ageing
The temperature of the containers during the ageing phase is controlled and less than or equal to 25 °C.
### 2° – Provisions relating to packaging
For every packaged lot, the operator keeps available for the approved control body:
- the information appearing in the register of handling operations referred to in Article D. 645-18 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code;
- the reports of analyses carried out before or after packaging.
These reports are kept for a period of 6 months from the date of packaging.
### 3° – Provisions relating to storage
The operator provides evidence of a protected storage place for wines packaged in bottles.
### 4° – Provisions relating to the circulation of products and placing on the market for the consumer
The wines are placed on the market for the consumer in accordance with the provisions of Article D. 645-17 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code.
## X. – Link with the geographical area
### 1° – Information on the geographical area
a) – Description of the natural factors contributing to the link
The geographical area occupies a group of hillsides nestled at the heart of the plateaux of the “Auxerrois”, in the Paris Basin. The “Chablisien” is a small, well-defined region corresponding to the reliefs bordering the valley of the Serein, a tributary of the Yonne, where it crosses the “Côte des Bars”. The geographical area thus extends over the territory of 17 communes of the Yonne department, east of Auxerre.
The cuesta of the “Côte des Bars”, a large geomorphological structure exposing Upper Jurassic sediments, is a relief formed of hard limestone, the “Calcaire du Barrois”, forming a cornice above a long slope whose subsoil consists of compact and impermeable marls (clayey limestones), the “Marnes à exogyra virgula”, rich in small comma-shaped oysters that have given their name to the formation. Below the marls, a limestone level forms a small topographic step near the valley floor (“Calcaire à Astartes”). The marls making up the slope are most often masked by a mantle of scree mixing fine materials and coarser limestone elements. The difference in height of the front of the côte reaches 120 metres to 130 metres, altitudes remaining moderate; the highest point of the plateau does not exceed 320 metres. In the north-east of the geographical area, the limestone plateau is overlain by Cretaceous formations, represented by oyster clays and marls.
The Chablis landscape is highly original. Large, slightly concave slopes occupied by the vine are dominated by a wooded limestone cornice that closes off the views, which open only along the axis of the Serein valley. A multitude of small valleys, often dry, cut hillsides with varied orientations. These valleys are generally steep-sided and wooded on their steepest faces, leaving room for the vine only on the best-exposed slopes.
The parcels delimited for the harvesting of the grapes occupy various situations:
- the hillsides, mainly on the marly slopes with well-drained soils;
- but also locally the piedmont on the “calcaires à Astartes” or the colluvium at the foot of the slopes;
- the edges of the plateaux, with shallow and very stony calcareous soils, ensuring good warming, which thus offsets the climatic deficit due to the lack of shelter.
The soils are varied, although always calcareous. Lean, very stony and well drained on the limestones of the plateau (known locally as “petites terres”), they are richer in clay on the marly slope and its piedmont. In the north-west of the geographical area, a few wine-growing sectors have developed on the Cretaceous clays covering the “calcaires du Barrois”. The soils are then impermeable and drainage is ensured by the slope.
The “Chablisien” enjoys an oceanic climate slightly modified by continental influences. This climate is characterised by a moderate and regular rainfall regime (only 650 millimetres per year at Auxerre), with no marked summer drought, and rather cool temperatures, with an annual average of 10.8 °C. The climate is characterised by a fairly high risk of winter and spring frosts, which can be catastrophic for the vine. The relatively steep-sided relief increases this risk in the valleys, while the plateaux are exposed to icy winter winds.
b) – Description of the human factors contributing to the link
The cultivation of the vine is attested in the “Chablisien” from the early Middle Ages. A monastery was founded in Chablis in 510, then ceded in 867 to the monks of Saint-Martin de Tours fleeing the Viking invasion of the Loire country. It seems that they developed a vineyard close to the village.
In 1114, the Cistercians founded the Abbey of Pontigny near Chablis and gradually developed a vineyard, then set up in the town of Chablis a cellar, the “Petit Pontigny”, whose buildings still exist. Alongside the ecclesiastical estates, which lasted until the French Revolution, a lay viticulture prospered, also turned towards external trade. Paris and northern Europe were regular outlets.
Throughout its history, the wine of “Chablis” has remained remarkably stable in its type of dry white wine. In 1186, a gift to the abbey of Pontigny concerned a vine located near Chablis, giving a wine that was “white, and long-keeping”. Nineteenth-century authors also noted this particularity, which made “Chablis” an exception in a wine world dominated by red and “clairet” wines.
Phylloxera, downy mildew and powdery mildew seriously affected the “Chablisien”, which took several decades to recover. Only in the second half of the 20th century, supported by progress in viticulture, mechanisation and frost protection, was the reconstitution of the vineyard truly relaunched. This reconstruction was carried out in keeping with usage, with a vineyard devoted solely to the production of dry white wines.
The use of the name “Petit Chablis” is attested from the beginning of the 20th century, when producers created their first syndicate, in 1908, in order to fight effectively against usurpations of the name. The name “Petit Chablis” appears in the various judgments recognising the controlled designation of origin “Chablis”, designating either dry white wines produced in the Chablis region from varieties other than “beaunois” (local name of chardonnay B – judgment of 1920), or wines from the “beaunois” variety but not originating from parcels with soils “of Kimmeridgian nature” (1923).
The controlled designation of origin “Petit Chablis” was recognised by decree in 1944.
“Petit Chablis” comes from a single variety, chardonnay B, renowned for producing the great white wines of Burgundy. The wines are produced mainly from vines planted on the plateaux with calcareous soils and, in the north-west, on slopes of Cretaceous clays. The lower slopes with heavier soils are also planted locally.
The “Petit Chablis” vineyard is traditionally trained with long pruning. The traditional pruning known as “Chablis pruning” is well suited to the difficult climatic conditions while ensuring regular and moderate production. Single or double Guyot pruning, which became widespread throughout the Burgundian vineyards in the 19th century, is also practised in the “Chablisien”. The development of high-performing plant material through clonal selection favours the development of short pruning allowing good regulation of the vegetation.
The vines are trellised and generally planted along the slope. While this arrangement helps the clay soils to drain, it also causes erosion problems, offset by a return to soil cultivation and grass cover.
In 2009 the vineyard covered an area of about 850 hectares, for an annual production of about 50,000 hectolitres.
### 2° – Information on the quality and characteristics of the products
“Petit Chablis” is a dry, lively and fruity white wine, characterised by its minerality and lightness. Its acidity and minerality give it a character of freshness and make it an early-drinking wine that does not need to age to be pleasant to drink.
### 3° – Causal interactions
The exclusively white wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin “Petit Chablis” owe their lively and mineral character to the cool oceanic climate of the geographical area. Mesoclimatic conditions shape the wine-growing potential, and the expression of the wines is particularly sensitive to vintage conditions.
The “Chablisien” occupies a particular place within Burgundy as a whole, through its relative isolation from the other great vineyards of Burgundy, as well as through the apparent simplicity of its “terroir”, with a simple geological context, a single variety and a single type of wine.
Over the generations, producers have developed specific know-how enabling them to get the best out of the chardonnay B variety in this difficult environment. For example, they have favoured pruning techniques that best regulate the yield and thus ensure optimal ripeness, and developed means of protection against frost.
“Petit Chablis” is produced mainly from vines planted on parcels situated on the cool plateau, on the lower slopes, or with clay soils. These natural situations favour a controlled ripening of the grapes, which guarantees sufficient acidity for the expression of the liveliness that characterises the wines.
The controlled designation of origin “Petit Chablis” is one of the components of the Chablis vineyard, alongside the controlled designations of origin “Chablis” and “Chablis grand cru”, with which it forms a range of wines that are close in their grape variety, environmental conditions and producers’ know-how. Each nevertheless has its own character. “Petit Chablis” appears as the lightest and liveliest. A wine to be drunk young, it stands apart in particular from “Chablis grand cru”, the wine for keeping par excellence.
## XI. – Transitional measures
Training methods
Vine parcels in place before 28 March 2003 which do not comply with the provisions relating to planting density and the spacing between rows set out in the present specification continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are [grubbed up].
## XII. – Rules of presentation and labelling
### 1° – General provisions
Wines for which, under the terms of the present specification, the controlled designation of origin “Petit Chablis” is claimed and which are presented under the said appellation may not be declared after the harvest, offered to the public, dispatched, put up for sale or sold unless, in the harvest declaration, in advertisements, on leaflets, labels, invoices and any containers whatsoever, the aforesaid controlled designation of origin is entered.
### 2° – Particular provisions
Where the grape variety is indicated on the labelling, this indication does not appear in the same field of vision as the compulsory particulars and is printed in characters whose dimensions do not exceed 2 millimetres.
