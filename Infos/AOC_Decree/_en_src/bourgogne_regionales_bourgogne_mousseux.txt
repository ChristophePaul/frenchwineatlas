# CHAPTER I
## I. – Name of the designation
Only wines meeting the specific provisions set out below may claim the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne mousseux”, initially recognised by the decree of 16 March 1943.
## II. – Geographical denominations and complementary terms
No particular provisions.
## III. – Colour and types of product
The controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne mousseux” is reserved for red sparkling wines.

## V. – Grape varieties
### 1° – Grape varieties
The wines come from the following varieties:
- main varieties: gamay N, pinot noir N and, for the Yonne department only, the césar N variety;
- accessory varieties: aligoté B, chardonnay B, gamay de Bouze N, gamay de Chaudenay N, melon B, pinot blanc B, pinot gris.
### 2° – Proportion rules on the holding
a) – Compliance of the planting is assessed over all the parcels of the holding producing the wine of the controlled designation of origin;
b) – The proportion of the accessory varieties, together or separately, is less than or equal to 10 % of the planting;
c) – The accessory varieties chardonnay B, pinot blanc B and pinot gris G are also authorised as mixed plantings within the vines; their total proportion is limited to 15 % within each parcel.
## VI. – Management of the vineyard
### 1° – Training methods
a) – Planting density
- The vines have a minimum planting density of 5,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows less than or equal to 2.50 metres;
- In the delimited vineyard of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” followed by the geographical denominations “Hautes Côtes de Beaune” and “Hautes Côtes de Nuits”, the vines have a minimum planting density of 4,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows less than or equal to 3 metres;
- Where the planting density is greater than 8,000 plants per hectare, the vines have a spacing between plants within the same row greater than or equal to 0.50 metre;
- Where the planting density is less than or equal to 8,000 plants per hectare, the vines have a spacing between plants within the same row greater than or equal to 0.80 metre.
Vines planted “en foule” (randomly, without rows) have a minimum planting density of 9,000 plants per hectare and a spacing between plants greater than 0.50 metre.
Vines trained according to the so-called “lyre” system have a minimum planting density of 3,000 plants per hectare, with a spacing between rows of less than 3.50 metres and a spacing between plants within the same row of between 0.80 metre and 1 metre.

d) – Average maximum load per parcel
The average maximum load per parcel is set at 10,000 kilograms per hectare.
e) – Threshold of missing vines
The percentage of dead or missing vine plants referred to in Article D. 645-4 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code is set at 20 %.
f) – Cultivation condition of the vine
The parcels are managed so as to ensure a good overall cultivation condition of the vine, in particular its sanitary condition and the upkeep of its soil.
### 2° – Other cultivation practices
a) – In order to preserve the characteristics of the physical and biological environment, which constitutes a fundamental element of the terroir:
- Permanent grass cover of the headlands is compulsory;
- Any substantial modification of the morphology, the subsoil, the arable layer or the elements that guarantee the integrity and sustainability of the soils of a parcel intended for the production of the controlled designation of origin is prohibited, with the exception of classic deep-ploughing works.
b) – Vine plantings and replacements are carried out with healthy plant material that has undergone hot-water treatment or any other method making it possible to combat flavescence dorée.
### 3° – Irrigation
Irrigation is prohibited.
## VII. – Harvest, transport and ripeness of the grapes
### 1° – Harvest
a) – The wines come from grapes harvested at good ripeness.
b) – Particular provisions for transport of the harvest
The harvest is protected from rain during its transport and on its reception.
### 2° – Ripeness of the grapes
a) – Sugar content of the grapes
Grapes with a sugar content of less than 171 grams per litre of must may not be considered to be at good ripeness.
b) – Minimum natural alcoholic strength by volume
Base wines intended for the production of sparkling wines have a minimum natural alcoholic strength by volume of 10 %.
## VIII. – Yields – Entry into production
### 1° – Yield
The yield referred to in Article D. 645-7 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code is set at 64 hectolitres per hectare.
### 2° – Limit yield
The limit yield referred to in Article D. 645-7 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code is set at 69 hectolitres per hectare.
### 3° – Entry into production of young vines
The benefit of the controlled designation of origin may only be granted to wines coming from:
- parcels of young vines only from the 2nd year following that in the course of which planting was carried out in place before 31 July;
- parcels of young vines only from the 1st year following that in the course of which grafting in place was carried out before 31 July;
- parcels of vines that have been top-grafted, at the earliest in the 1st year following that in the course of which the top-grafting was carried out before 31 July, and once the parcels no longer contain any varieties other than those permitted for the appellation. By derogation, in the year following that in the course of which the top-grafting was carried out before 31 July, the varieties permitted for the appellation may represent only 80 % of the planting of each parcel concerned.
## IX. – Processing, elaboration, ageing, packaging and storage
### 1° – General provisions
The wines are vinified in accordance with local, fair and constant usages.
a) – Blending of varieties
- The proportion of the accessory varieties, together or separately, is less than or equal to 49 % in the cuvée;
- “Cuvée” means all the volumes of wine intended directly for bottling for the prise de mousse; it consists of one base wine or a blend of base wines;
- Base wines produced from parcels planted with white varieties are vinified by blending the grapes concerned.
b) – Malolactic fermentation
Base wines have a maximum malic acid content of 0.4 gram per litre.
c) – Analytical standards
- Base wines have a maximum fermentable sugar content (glucose + fructose) of 2 grams per litre;
- After disgorging, the wines have a carbon dioxide overpressure of at least 3.5 atmospheres, measured at a temperature of 20 °C.
d) – Oenological practices and physical treatments
- Subtractive enrichment techniques (TSE) are authorised within the limit of a concentration rate of 10 %;
- The use of pieces of wood is prohibited;
- Where the must has been enriched, the sparkling wines do not exceed, before the addition of the dosage liqueur (liqueur d’expédition), a total alcoholic strength by volume of 13.5 %.
f) – Prohibited equipment
Continuous presses are prohibited.
g) – Vat capacity
Every operator has an overall vat capacity (vinification and storage) equivalent to at least 150 % of the volume of wine vinified for the previous year’s harvest, as entered either on the harvest declaration, pro rata to the change in the holding’s area in production, or on the production declaration.
h) – Upkeep of the cellar and equipment
The cellar and the equipment are well maintained; this is reflected in particular by:
- general hygiene of the elaboration premises, with general cleanliness, well-maintained floors, adequate drainage and a surface avoiding stagnation;
- harmlessness of the equipment and products coming into contact with the wine;
- separation and specificity of the premises: premises without the same functions must be separated, such as storage areas for plant-protection products, cleaning products or hydrocarbons, from the premises for vinification, ageing and storage of dry goods (corks, cartons);
- management of wine effluents: effluents must be removed as quickly as possible from foodstuff premises and deposited in well-maintained containers that are easy to clean and can be closed; waste storage areas must be kept clean; waste must be disposed of hygienically and in an environmentally friendly manner; a waste storage and removal area must be provided;
- absence of hazardous or odorous substances in the vinification, ageing and storage premises (odour).
i) – Temperature control (vinification of the base wines)
The temperature of the containers during the vinification of the base wines is controlled and less than or equal to 25 °C.
### 2° – Provisions by type of product
a) – The wines are produced exclusively by second fermentation in glass bottles.
b) – Bottling (tirage) in the bottles in which the prise de mousse takes place is carried out from 1 December following the harvest.
c) – The period of keeping in bottle on lees may not be less than 9 months.
### 3° – Provisions relating to packaging
a) – For every packaged lot, the operator keeps available for the approved control body:
- the information appearing in the register of handling operations referred to in Article D. 645-18 of the Rural and Maritime Fishing Code;
- the reports of analyses carried out after the prise de mousse. The analysis reports are kept for a period of 6 months from the date of tirage.
b) – The wines are produced and sold in the bottle in which the prise de mousse took place, except for wines sold in bottles with a volume of 37.5 centilitres or less, or of more than 150 centilitres.
### 4° – Provisions relating to storage
The operator provides evidence of a protected storage place for packaged wines, in particular products packaged in unlabelled bottles, with a temperature of between 5 °C and 22 °C.
### 5° – Provisions relating to the circulation of products and placing on the market for the consumer
a) – Date of placing on the market for the consumer
The wines are placed on the market for the consumer after a minimum ageing period of 9 months from the date of tirage.
b) – Period during which the wines may not circulate between authorised warehousekeepers
The wines circulate between authorised warehousekeepers after a minimum period of 9 months from the date of tirage.
## X. – Link with the geographical area
### 1° – Information on the geographical area
a) – Description of the natural factors contributing to the link
The geographical area rests on the reliefs traditionally devoted to viticulture in the Yonne, Côte-d’Or, Saône-et-Loire and Rhône departments. It groups together a more or less separate set of vineyards stretching for about 250 kilometres from north to south. It thus extends over the territory of more than 300 communes. Each of the vineyards is characterised by its own landscapes, geology and climate.
The vineyards of the Yonne and of the “Châtillonnais”, north of the “Côte d’Or”, are planted on the cuestas of the Paris Basin, on sediments dated to the Upper Jurassic, with the exception of the “Vézelien”, planted on Lower and Middle Jurassic formations, and the “Jovinien”, planted on Upper Cretaceous formations. The substrata are mainly marly (clayey limestones), locally calcareous. The vineyard is confined to the best-exposed reliefs of the cuestas, on the flanks of the main valleys draining the region, the Yonne and its tributaries, the Seine and its tributaries. It lies at altitudes of between 150 metres and 300 metres.
From Dijon to the outskirts of Lyon, the vineyards occupy an almost continuous series of linear reliefs. This is the western edge of the Bresse rift, a major tectonic structure that subsided during the Alpine uplift. The substrata are mainly sedimentary, limestones or marls, generally dated to the Jurassic, but also locally to the Triassic. Locally, mainly in Saône-et-Loire, outcrops of Primary-era metamorphic and granitic basement, generating acid soils, bear vines. Although the reliefs of the hinterland are sometimes high (650 metres in Côte-d’Or, 1,000 metres in the “Beaujolais”), the vines are confined to altitudes of between 250 metres and 400 metres.
Each of the vineyards is characterised by its own landscapes, geology and morphological style:
- broad valleys cutting wedge-like into the limestone plateaux of the Paris Basin;
- a thin continuous strip lying on the boundary between plain and plateau for “the Côte”;
- a series of parallel reliefs stretching on a north–south axis, the “chaînons” (ridges), in the “Mâconnais”.
“Wine-growing Burgundy” enjoys a rather cool oceanic climate. This climate is characterised by a moderate and regular rainfall regime, with no marked summer drought. Temperatures reflect its coolness, with an annual average of 11 °C. Well marked in the Yonne department, it is somewhat attenuated in the south-east of the geographical area by the screening role played by the relief of the Morvan and Charolais hills, which generates a foehn effect expressed in lower humidity and higher temperatures than the regional reference. The eastern part of the geographical area is affected by continental influences, expressed in relatively low winter temperatures and periods of frost that can be long and intense, but also in autumns that are sometimes very dry and sunny. Southern influences, perceptible above all in the southern part of the geographical area, can momentarily generate high summer temperatures and rising warm sea air, responsible for summer storms.
The parcels delimited for the harvesting of the grapes correspond to all the sectors traditionally recognised for their aptitude for viticulture. They thus preferentially occupy the well-exposed hillsides of the main reliefs, as well as the piedmonts, the cool plateaux and the back slopes facing north and west, all situations ensuring optimal drainage and good soil-warming capacity.
b) – Description of the human factors contributing to the link
The cultivation of the vine is attested from the Gallo-Roman period. In the Middle Ages, the vineyards of “Burgundy” had already acquired economic importance and were known beyond its borders. Auxerre produced the so-called “basse Bourgogne” wines, while the “Côte-d’Or” and part of the “Côte châlonnaise” were known for supplying the “Vins de Beaune”. The role of ecclesiastical structures (abbeys, bishoprics) and of the nobility in building the renown of the wines of “Burgundy” is well known. Cluny, Cîteaux, Pontigny, the Chapter of Langres and the Dukes of Burgundy each contributed in their own way to building a prestigious vineyard and to the influence of these wines.
In the 18th century, the significant development of the wine trade generated a new economic power. The wines of “Burgundy” were widely sold to the north of France and of Europe, and even to other continents.
In the 18th century, the significant development of the wine trade generated a new economic power: the wines of Burgundy were widely exported to the north of France and of Europe, and even to other continents.
However, wine-growing “Burgundy” became clearly individualised during the 19th century. Old (provinces) or recent (departments) administrative divisions combined with the economic dimension, with urban centres such as Auxerre, Dijon, Beaune, Chalon-sur-Saône or Mâcon ensuring the distribution of the region’s wines. The north of the “Beaujolais” identified itself at that time with the wines of Mâcon. The “Tonnerrois” and the “Châtillonnais”, at the gates of Champagne, also turned towards Burgundy.
At the beginning of the 19th century, producers in the Burgundy region took an interest in making sparkling wines according to the techniques developed in Champagne, particularly at Rully, in Saône-et-Loire, from 1820, at Nuits-Saint-Georges, then, in 1840, at Chablis. The new product was successful, especially with customers in England and the United States, and the method spread rapidly. Sparkling wines were made at the heart of the finest territories, such as the “Clos Vougeot”, “Chambertin”, “Chablis”, …
The producers of sparkling wines joined together in a syndicate in 1939 and obtained, in 1943, recognition of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne mousseux”. The decree reserved this controlled designation of origin for white, red and rosé wines produced by second fermentation in bottle. From the 1960s, Burgundian producers wishing to formalise and protect their production of quality sparkling wines put in place strict harvesting and production rules, which led to the recognition of the controlled designation of origin “Crémant de Bourgogne” by a decree of 1975, for white or rosé wines. From 1984, the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne mousseux” was then reserved for red wines only.
Sparkling wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne mousseux” are produced from the region’s traditional black varieties, pinot noir N and gamay N. According to usage, some white varieties may be present, mixed in the vine parcels or in the blend making up the cuvées, in minimal proportions. The geographical dispersion of the vineyard is accompanied by some differences in production methods. Each small region, inheriting ancient know-how, has thus preserved local practices in the training of the vine, as shown by the arched canes in the Mâconnais or the so-called “Chablis” pruning in the Yonne.
### 2° – Information on the quality and characteristics of the product
The wine mainly expresses fruity aromas, depending on the variety most present in the cuvée. It thus offers nuances ranging from small red and black fruits to notes of cherry or strawberry. It is characterised by its aromatic power. Freshness on the palate is enhanced by the release of carbon dioxide.
### 3° – Causal interactions
“Bourgogne” is a northern vineyard where the vine is subject to strong climatic constraint. Planting is therefore confined to the most favourable situations, characterised by reliefs enjoying both mesoclimates that are warmer and drier than the regional climate and free-draining soils able to remove excess water while offering good fertility and rapid warming.
Within these privileged sites, the parcels delimited for the harvesting of the grapes intended for the production of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne mousseux” occupy all situations and extend to sites suited to preserving a certain freshness, in particular piedmonts with relatively heavy soils, or plateaux and back slopes with a cool, well-ventilated climate.
The cool character of the climate of the geographical area is particularly favourable to the production of sparkling wines. Soils and exposures combine in sites which, while ensuring good ripeness of the grapes, preserve the liveliness necessary for the quality of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne mousseux”. A little-diversified, indigenous planting, particularly well suited to local soil and climate conditions, contributes to the expression of a range of wines sharing the acidity and fruit of northern wines, giving them undeniable elegance.
The current production of sparkling wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne mousseux” is very small. It nevertheless represents a historic component of Burgundian wine production.
## XI. – Transitional measures
### 1° – Grape varieties
Vine parcels in place on 31 July 2009 and planted with the tressot N variety continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up.
### 2° – Training methods
a) – Vine parcels in place on 31 July 2009, situated in the communes of the Rhône and Saône-et-Loire departments belonging to the delimited vineyard of the controlled designation of origin “Beaujolais”, with a density of between 4,000 and 5,000 plants per hectare, continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up.
b) – Vine parcels in place on 31 July 2009, situated in the Yonne department, with a density of less than 5,000 plants per hectare continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up.
c) – Vine parcels in place before 1980, situated outside the delimited vineyard of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” followed by the geographical denominations “Hautes Côtes de Beaune” and “Hautes Côtes de Nuits”, which do not comply with the provisions relating to planting density set out in the present specification and are trained on a “single-plane” trellis, continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up, provided that they:
- have a planting density greater than 3,000 plants per hectare;
- are pruned with a maximum of 6 free eyes per square metre;
- have an external leaf-canopy surface of at least 1.50 square metres for the production of one kilogram of grapes.
The volume that may be claimed under the controlled designation of origin is calculated on the basis of the yield authorised for the controlled designation of origin multiplied by a coefficient of 0.80.
d) – Vine parcels in place before the date of approval of the present specification, situated in the delimited vineyard of the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne” followed by the geographical denominations “Hautes Côtes de Beaune” and “Hautes Côtes de Nuits”, which do not comply with the provisions relating to planting density set out in the present specification and are trained on a “single-plane” trellis, continue to benefit, for their harvest, from the right to the controlled designation of origin until they are grubbed up, provided that they:
- have a planting density greater than 3,000 plants per hectare;
- are pruned with a maximum of 6 free eyes per square metre;
- have an external leaf-canopy surface of at least 1.50 square metres for the production of one kilogram of grapes.
## XII. – Rules of presentation and labelling
Wines for which, under the terms of the present specification, the controlled designation of origin “Bourgogne mousseux” is claimed and which are presented under the said appellation may not be declared after the harvest, offered to the public, dispatched, put up for sale or sold unless, in the harvest declaration, in advertisements, on leaflets, labels, invoices and any containers whatsoever, the aforesaid controlled designation of origin is entered.
# CHAPTER II
## I. – Declaration obligations
#### 1. Claim declaration for base wines
The claim declaration is sent to the defence and management body at least fifteen days before circulation of the wines between authorised warehousekeepers and at the latest on 10 December of the year of harvest. It indicates in particular:
- the designation claimed;
- the volume of the base wine;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the name and address of the applicant;
- the place of storage of the base wine.
It is accompanied by a copy of the harvest declaration and, as the case may be, a copy of the production declaration or an extract of the stock accounts for buyers of grapes, musts or base wines.
#### 2. Declaration prior to transaction and collections for base wines
Every operator wishing to move base wines sends the defence and management body and the approved control body a transaction declaration for the lot concerned between the day the transaction is contracted and at least eight days before collection. If the volume actually collected differs by more or less than 10 % from the volume indicated at the time of contracting, the operator makes a correction available to the approved control body.
#### 3. So-called “end of tirage” claim declaration
The claim declaration is sent to the defence and management body at the latest at the end of the month in which the tirage was carried out. It indicates:
- the designation claimed;
- the volume of wine, expressed in number of bottles;
- the tirage number;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the name and address of the applicant;
- the place of storage of the wine.
It is accompanied by an extract of the stock accounts for buyers of grapes, musts or base wines, and by the general plan of the storage places.
#### 4. Downgrading declaration
Every operator carrying out a downgrading of wines benefiting from the controlled designation of origin sends the defence and management body and the approved control body a monthly declaration within time limits set in the control plan. This declaration indicates in particular:
- the identity of the operator;
- the EVV or SIRET number;
- the volume concerned by the downgrading;
- the vintage, where applicable;
- the date of downgrading.
#### 5. Declaration of equipment for TSE
Every operator holding a concentration apparatus declares it upon purchase to the defence and management body, specifying its specifications. The defence and management body keeps an up-to-date list of operators holding such equipment and sends it each year to the services of the National Institute of Origin and Quality at the latest on 1 September.
Every operator using a service provider declares it to the defence and management body, which draws up the list of these operators and sends it each year to the services of the National Institute of Origin and Quality at the latest on 1 September.
#### 6. Reworking of parcels
Before any development or any works liable to modify the morphology, the subsoil, the arable layer (including any addition of exogenous soil) or the elements that guarantee the integrity and sustainability of the soils of a parcel intended for the production of the controlled designation of origin, with the exception of classic deep-ploughing works, the operator sends a declaration to the defence and management body at least four weeks before the planned start date of the works. The defence and management body transmits, without delay, a copy of this declaration to the services of the National Institute of Origin and Quality.
## II. – Keeping of registers
The registers provided for in the present specification are kept and held available to the agents responsible for control, and can be communicated to them on prior request. They may be kept in any form (paper or electronic).
#### 1. General plan of the storage and vinification places
Every vinifying operator keeps up to date and available for the approved control body a general plan of the storage and vinification places, allowing in particular the number, designation and capacity of the containers to be identified.
#### 2. TSE register
Every operator carrying out partial concentration of musts keeps an up-to-date TSE register including in particular:
- the initial volume;
- the volume of water evaporated;
- the identification of the lot after concentration (volume and potential alcoholic strength).
#### 3. Tirage register
Keeping a tirage register is compulsory. This register specifies:
- the start date of tirage;
- the end date of tirage;
- the volume used;
- the composition of the cuvée;
- the tirage number.
#### 4. Disgorging register
Every operator keeps an up-to-date register for each disgorging operation. This register indicates in particular:
- the start date of the operation;
- the tirage number(s) of the lot(s) concerned with the corresponding volume;
- the tirage date(s);
- the volume of wine, expressed in number of bottles, after disgorging;
- the lot number after disgorging.
