In the winemaker's words

Making Red Wine

After the harvest, the grapes are separated from the stalks and sorted — manually or mechanically — to remove unwanted fruit and other material. The fruit is then crushed and usually destemmed. At that point a little sulphur may be added to prevent early oxidation of the must, which is then pumped into stainless-steel tanks or wooden vats (foudres).

For red wine, skin contact and alcoholic fermentation happen together in the same vat — a stage the French call cuvaison. Over several days to a few weeks the cap of skins is kept in contact with the juice, by pigeage (punching down) or remontage (pumping over), to draw out colour and tannin. When fermentation finishes, the free-run vin de goutte is run off and the leftover marc is pressed to yield vin de presse, which may or may not be blended back.

The wine then rests while malolactic fermentation softens its acidity. A vin de garde may be barrel-aged (élevage) for up to about 24 months before it is fined — often with egg white (collage) — and/or filtered, then bottled.

Making White Wine

The huge variety of white wines reflects many different vinifications, each adapted to a particular wine. White winemaking differs from red at several stages — above all, the skins are rarely macerated with the juice. Instead the grapes are usually pressed straight away and only the juice is fermented.

After pressing, the cloudy juice is left to settle so the solids drop out — a clarification step called débourbage. Fermentation then runs cooler than for red, to protect delicate aromas. Most whites are not barrel-aged, though some Grands Vins — the grands crus of Burgundy, the classified wines of Bordeaux — are raised in oak. Malolactic fermentation is not automatic for white wine but is increasingly used for the Grands Vins. A final fining (often with bentonite) and clarification precede bottling.

Making Sweet & Late-Harvest Wine

Sweet wines keep sugar in the finished wine by starting with far riper, more concentrated grapes and then stopping fermentation before the yeast has eaten all the sugar. Concentration can come from Botrytis cinerea (Noble Rot), which shrivels the berries — as in Sauternes, the Loire's Quarts-de-Chaume, Alsace Sélection de Grains Nobles, or Tokaji — from drying on the vine (passerillage), or from freezing (ice wine / Eiswein).

Fermentation is halted by chilling, by adding sulphur dioxide, and by removing the yeast through centrifugation, pasteurisation or racking. The resulting wines are extremely rich — roughly 50–150 g/L of residual sugar. (A related family, the vins doux naturels such as Muscat de Beaumes-de-Venise, instead stop fermentation by adding grape spirit — a step called mutage.)

Making Rosé

Rosé takes its colour from brief skin contact, not from blending red and white (forbidden for still wine in the EU, with Champagne the notable exception). Two main methods: direct press, where red grapes are pressed almost like whites for a very pale colour (the Provence style); and saignée ("bleeding"), where a little pink juice is run off a red-wine vat after a few hours of maceration, giving a deeper colour — and incidentally concentrating the red wine left behind. Fermentation and the rest follow the white-wine road.
